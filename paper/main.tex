\affil[1]{Department of Physics, Virginia Tech, Blacksburg, Virginia 24061, USA}

\begin{abstract}
We introduce \toolname, an executable checker that turns proposed
four-dimensional $\mathcal N=1$ Seiberg-duality data into auditable
\emph{consistency certificates}.  A certificate records the result of exact
checks for anomaly matching, superpotential $R$-charge constraints, central
charges computed from the encoded $R$-charges, and a bounded chiral-ring
comparison.  It is deliberately weaker than a proof of infrared duality.  We
use this checker as the acceptance predicate in a pre-specified repair
benchmark of 145 one-edit perturbations, nested in 14 theory--node bases.  On
two confirmatory language models, allowing as many as five generic retries
instead of one attempt increased certified repair by $8.3\pp$ for \dscode{} and
$7.1\pp$ for \qwcode{} (paper-wide Holm-adjusted $p=0.0015$ and $0.0016$).
Under an equal 11-call cap, the frozen strategy portfolio underperformed
independent verifier-filtered resampling by $10.3\pp$ on \dscode{} but
outperformed it by $14.7\pp$ on \qwcode{}, reversing the ordering of the two
tested verifier-exploitation policies across the two confirmatory models.
Content-bearing verifier feedback gave narrower, model-contingent gains.  The
result is therefore not a universal ranking of prompting strategies: it is
evidence that an exact, inexpensive certificate can support different search
policies while preserving a common, auditable success criterion.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Formal environments have made an important class of mathematical reasoning
machine-interactive: a proposed proof step produces an exact state transition
and an exact acceptance or rejection signal.  Systems such as LeanDojo expose
this interface to learning agents, and AlphaProof demonstrates how a formal
checker can anchor large-scale search at difficult mathematical problems
\cite{Yang:2023leandojo,Hubert:2026alphaproof}.  Most theoretical-physics
claims, however, are not already encoded as theorem-prover goals.  The useful
intermediate question is therefore not whether a language model can prove a
physical equivalence, but whether part of the claim can be compiled into a
typed object with explicit, machine-checkable obligations.

Seiberg duality provides a concrete test case.  It relates distinct ultraviolet
descriptions that are conjectured to flow to the same infrared physics
\cite{Seiberg:1994pq,Intriligator:1995au}.  Proposed dual pairs obey demanding
necessary conditions---including 't Hooft anomaly matching, compatible
superpotentials, and matching central charges---yet passing such tests is not a
proof of duality.  Toric quiver theories offer structured examples in which
Seiberg-dual phases and superpotentials can be represented symbolically
\cite{Feng:2001bn}.  This makes them suitable for studying a deliberately
narrow interface: can an exact checker detect a corrupted claim, expose a
useful failure state, and certify a repaired claim without pretending to settle
the underlying infrared equivalence?

We answer this question with \toolname.  The system accepts a pair of encoded
quiver gauge theories, evaluates a fixed set of obligations, and emits a
certificate containing the result of each check together with assumptions,
conventions, and limitations.  We then deliberately perturb known
machine-generated dual pairs and ask language models to repair the corrupted
magnetic theory.  The final checker is held fixed, models cannot modify its
settings, and every accepted output must pass a stricter final check than the
feedback-time check.

The paper has three empirical rungs, ordered from the least to the most
strategy-specific.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.1em,itemsep=0.25em]
  \item \textbf{Certificate substrate.}  We define an executable consistency
  interface for a scoped class of Seiberg-duality claims.  This is an auditable
  necessary-condition checker, not a proof system for duality.
  \item \textbf{Iteration plus exact filtering.}  On both confirmatory models,
  as many as five failure-only generic retries improve certified repair over a single
  attempt.  This endpoint bundles a larger call budget with exact rejection;
  it does not isolate a causal effect of feedback.
  \item \textbf{Search-policy reversal under an equal call cap.}  A frozen
  sequential portfolio and independent best-of-11 resampling have opposite
  orderings on the two tested models.  Hence neither policy should be reported
  as universally optimal.
\end{enumerate}

Two narrower controls ask whether the content of the verifier response matters.
They support that conclusion for \qwcode{} but not for \dscode{}.  Across all
comparisons, the invariant is the certificate: whichever search policy is used,
success has the same exact and inspectable definition.

\paragraph{Relation to verifier-guided reasoning.}
Candidate generation followed by a learned verifier can improve answer
selection in mathematical word problems \cite{Cobbe:2021verifiers}; formal
autoformalization can instead filter candidates using a proof assistant's
internal consistency checks \cite{Zhou:2024verify}.  These are materially
different forms of verification.  \toolname{} is a deterministic,
domain-specific symbolic checker, neither a learned reward model nor a general
proof assistant.  Work on intrinsic self-correction also cautions against
assuming that a model can reliably repair its own reasoning without an external
signal \cite{Huang:2024selfcorrect}.  Our experiments isolate several ways of
using such a signal, rather than treating ``verifier feedback'' as a single
intervention.

\section{Consistency certificates for duality claims}
\label{sec:certificates}

\subsection{Claim schema and certificate semantics}

An input claim contains two fully specified gauge-theory descriptions, called
Theory A and Theory B.  Each description records gauge nodes and ranks, chiral
fields and representations, global and $R$-charges, and a polynomial
superpotential.  The representation is intentionally explicit: the checker
does not infer missing matter, repair syntax, or reinterpret conventions.  A
model participating in the repair benchmark must return a complete replacement
for Theory B; Theory A is immutable.

For each obligation $o$, the checker returns a named status and diagnostic
payload.  The certificate is the tuple
\begin{equation}
  C=(\mathcal O,\,s,\,A,\,K,\,L),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal O$ is the ordered obligation set, $s(o)$ is the result of each
obligation, $A$ records assumptions, $K$ records conventions, and $L$ records
known limitations.  The outward-facing result is either an in-scope consistency
certificate, a failed obligation set, or an out-of-scope/invalid result.  In
particular,
\begin{equation}
  C=\textsc{certified}\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
  \text{Theory A and Theory B are proven infrared duals}.
\end{equation}
It means only that the supplied claim passed every enabled, finite obligation
under the recorded assumptions.

\subsection{Enabled obligations}
\label{sec:obligations}

The frozen repair profile enables four obligation families.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.7cm,style=nextline,itemsep=0.35em]
  \item[Anomalies.] Gauge-cubic consistency and selected mixed gauge--global
  anomalies are checked within each theory; matched global 't Hooft anomaly
  coefficients are then compared across the proposed pair.
  \item[Superpotential.] Every encoded monomial must be gauge-admissible under
  the implemented representation rules and have total $R$-charge two.  The
  check uses the charges supplied in the claim rather than performing
  $a$-maximization.
  \item[Central charges.] The anomaly traces of the encoded fermionic
  $R$-charges are converted to
  \begin{equation}
    a=\frac{3}{32}\!\left(3\operatorname{Tr}R^3-\operatorname{Tr}R\right),
    \qquad
    c=\frac{1}{32}\!\left(9\operatorname{Tr}R^3-5\operatorname{Tr}R\right),
  \end{equation}
  and compared between the two descriptions \cite{Anselmi:1997am}.  These are
  conditional values for the supplied $R$-assignment, not an independent
  determination of the superconformal $R$-symmetry.
  \item[Bounded chiral ring.] Cyclic single-trace words are enumerated up to a
  fixed cutoff, quotiented by the implemented F-term relations and cyclic
  rotations, and compared block by block.  The construction is finite and
  classical: it does not include quantum corrections, all trace identities, or
  operators beyond the cutoff.
\end{description}

The bounded chiral-ring calculation builds cyclic paths from the quiver,
computes cyclic derivatives of the superpotential, closes those relations under
admissible left and right contexts, and compares quotient dimensions in each
tested degree.  The frozen profile uses $R$-degree when singlets make path
length an unsuitable grading and path length otherwise.  A match is therefore
a necessary finite-cutoff check, not a claim that the full chiral rings are
isomorphic.

\subsection{Two checker depths and an immutable acceptance rule}

Interactive policies receive a bounded chiral-ring check through degree
$L_{\mathrm{fb}}=3$.  Final acceptance is recomputed from scratch using
$L_{\mathrm{final}}=5$.  The stricter second pass prevents a candidate from
being accepted merely because it overfits the visible feedback horizon.  A
candidate that fails to parse, copies Theory A verbatim, changes verifier
settings, or leaves the checker scope receives no certificate.  The model never
edits code or executes the verifier; it only proposes structured theory data.

This interface separates three roles that are often conflated:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.29\textwidth}p{0.38\textwidth}}
\toprule
Component & Role in the experiment & What it does not establish \\
\midrule
Generator & Proposes a repaired Theory B & Correctness or physical equivalence \\
Feedback view & Selects which diagnostic text, if any, is returned & A different success criterion \\
Final verifier & Applies one immutable acceptance predicate & Sufficiency of the checked conditions \\
Certificate log & Preserves obligations, states, and decisions & A formal proof term \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Frozen repair benchmark and experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Fixtures}

The benchmark contains 145 repairable depth-one fixtures generated with seed
20260715.  They are nested in 14 theory--node bases drawn from six toric-quiver
families: $dP_0$, $dP_1$, $dP_2$, $F_0$, SPP, and
$\mathbb C^3/(\mathbb Z_2\!\times\!\mathbb Z_2)$.  Starting from a valid
machine-generated dual pair, the fixture generator applies exactly one
perturbation to Theory B.  The locked set contains 38 dropped
superpotential terms, 13 superpotential-sign flips, 52 $R$-charge
perturbations, and 42 rank perturbations.  Every fixture is regenerated and
screened before use: the unperturbed pair must pass the final checker, the
perturbed pair must fail, and the target repair must be recoverable by the
allowed output schema.

The number 145 counts perturbations, not 145 statistically independent physical
theories.  The fixed benchmark is a controlled software test bed with repeated
variants of shared bases.  It supports comparisons on these named fixtures; it
does not by itself estimate performance over a population of quiver theories.

\subsection{Policies}
\label{sec:policies}

Each model receives the same serialized fixture and full-theory output contract.
The five policies differ only in call allocation and the diagnostic content
returned after a failed candidate.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=0.35em]
  \item[\ssarm.] One candidate is sampled and final-checked.
  \item[\grarm.] As many as five candidates are sampled.  After a failure the
  model receives a generic message that the proposed theory did not pass and
  should be revised, but no obligation identity.
  \item[\vfarm.] The same five-call limit is used, but a failed round returns
  the names and coarse categories of failed obligations.
  \item[\maskedarm.] The same interaction structure as \vfarm{} is retained,
  while obligation names are replaced by neutral positional placeholders.
  This controls for the presence and shape of a diagnostic list, but not for
  every possible attention effect of the placeholder tokens.
  \item[\bonarm.] Eleven independent candidates are drawn from the original
  prompt and passed through the final checker; the first certified candidate is
  selected.  No failure information is fed into later draws.
\end{description}

The E4 portfolio is a deterministic replay of independently collected
\ssarm{}, \grarm{}, and \vfarm{} trajectories in the frozen order
$\ssarm\rightarrow\grarm\rightarrow\vfarm$, stopping at the first final
certificate or after 11 calls.  It is compared with \bonarm{} under the same
maximum number of calls.  Because response lengths and early stopping differ,
this is a call-cap match, not a token-, latency-, or dollar-cost match.

\subsection{Endpoints and estimand}

The binary outcome $Y_{imr}$ is one if fixture $i$, model $m$, and replication
$r$ end with a final certificate.  Three confirmatory endpoints form the main
family:
\begin{align*}
  \mathrm{E1}&:\ \vfarm-\grarm &&\text{(category-level feedback content)},\\
  \mathrm{E2}&:\ \grarm-\ssarm &&\text{(iteration plus exact filtering)},\\
  \mathrm{E4}&:\ \text{portfolio}-\bonarm
    &&\text{(two verifier-exploitation policies at 11 calls)}.
\end{align*}
E5 compares \vfarm{} with \maskedarm{} and has a separate two-hypothesis
family.  E3 compares their first-round outcomes descriptively, before either arm
can receive feedback.

For each model and endpoint we fit a logit-link generalized estimating equation
with arm and replication fixed effects, fixture clusters, exchangeable working
correlation, and a robust sandwich covariance estimator
\cite{Liang:1986gee}.  We report standardized marginal risk differences in
percentage points with robust 95\% confidence intervals.  The six tests in
$\{\mathrm{E1},\mathrm{E2},\mathrm{E4}\}\times\{\dscode,\qwcode\}$ receive
one paper-wide Holm adjustment \cite{Holm:1979multiple}; E5 is adjusted within
its own two-model family.  Each arm has three complete replications over all 145
fixtures.

The protocol, prompts, fixture hashes, model aliases, stopping rules, and
analysis code were pre-specified and git-frozen before confirmatory response
collection (protocol commit \texttt{813fdb0}; execution-manifest commit
\texttt{c155cff}).  We use ``git-frozen'' rather than ``pre-registered'': the
record is public and immutable at the cited commits, but it was not deposited in
an external registration service.  The provider model names are rolling API
aliases queried in July 2026, not immutable weight hashes.

\section{Confirmatory results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Generic retry improves over one attempt on both models}

% claim:C3@supported
The broadest replicated result is E2.  Relative to \ssarm{}, \grarm{} increased
the standardized certified-repair rate by $8.3\pp$ for \dscode{} (95\% CI
$[3.8,12.8]$, Holm-adjusted $p=0.0015$) and by $7.1\pp$ for \qwcode{} (95\%
CI $[3.2,11.1]$, Holm-adjusted $p=0.0016$).  This comparison establishes the
value of the tested \emph{iteration-and-filtering bundle}: \grarm{} receives as
many as five model calls whereas \ssarm{} receives one.  It does not identify a
feedback-only mechanism or hold compute fixed.

\subsection{Equal-call policies reverse order across models}

% claim:C4@supported
E4 changes the comparison while holding the maximum number of model calls at
11.  On \dscode{}, the sequential portfolio was worse than independent
verifier-filtered resampling by $10.3\pp$ (95\% CI $[-16.2,-4.5]$,
Holm-adjusted $p=0.0016$).  On \qwcode{}, the same frozen portfolio was better
by $14.7\pp$ (95\% CI $[8.4,21.0]$, Holm-adjusted $p<10^{-4}$).  Figure
\ref{fig:e4} displays the reversal; Table~\ref{tab:primary} reports every frozen
endpoint.

The result is an interaction between the named policy implementations and the
two named model endpoints.  It does not license a taxonomy in which one model
is intrinsically a ``sampler'' and the other a ``feedback follower,'' nor does
it prove that either allocation is optimal.  The portfolio changes prompt
content, sequential dependence, and call allocation together.  E4 therefore
supports an existence claim---a policy ordering can reverse across models---not
a general model classification.

\subsection{Diagnostic content has a narrower effect}

% claim:C5@supported
E1 and E5 probe the content of the failure message.  For \dscode{},
category-level verifier feedback did not improve on generic retry (E1:
$-1.8\pp$, 95\% CI $[-6.6,3.0]$, adjusted $p=0.4528$), and the contrast with
structurally matched masked feedback was exactly $0.0\pp$ (E5: 95\% CI
$[-4.6,4.6]$, adjusted $p=1$).  For \qwcode{}, \vfarm{} improved on
\grarm{} by $8.7\pp$ (95\% CI $[3.8,13.7]$, adjusted $p=0.0016$) and on
\maskedarm{} by $6.4\pp$ (95\% CI $[2.2,10.7]$, adjusted $p=0.0065$).
First-round E3 differences were small and are descriptive only.  Thus the
obligation labels were useful in one tested model--interface pair, but the
shared E2 improvement cannot be attributed to diagnostic content alone.

Taken together, the three rungs distinguish a stable substrate from unstable
policy rankings.  The exact certificate made retry, content-bearing repair, and
independent resampling directly comparable.  Which of those uses performed best
depended on the model endpoint and call allocation; what counted as a successful
repair did not.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What has been shown.}
On a fixed, git-frozen benchmark, an executable necessary-condition checker can
serve as a common acceptance predicate for several language-model search
policies.  More calls plus exact filtering beat one call on both tested models,
and two equal-call policies changed order across those models.  The main result
is therefore about the experimental value of a machine-checkable substrate and
the danger of universal policy claims, not about automated discovery of new
dualities.

\paragraph{Certificates are not proofs.}
The implemented obligations are necessary and scoped.  In particular, the
chiral-ring comparison is bounded and classical, the central charges are
conditional on encoded $R$-charges, and the checker does not establish
renormalization-group flow, accidental symmetries, a superconformal index
identity, or a full operator map.  False positives relative to physical duality
can therefore pass.  Conversely, a true duality outside the representation or
convention set can be rejected as out of scope.

\paragraph{Benchmark scope.}
The 145 fixtures are one-edit perturbations of 14 shared bases in six families.
They measure repair on those perturbations, not independent discovery and not
population-level accuracy over four-dimensional quantum field theories.  The
generated targets and exact corruption classes may also favor localized edits.
Harder multi-edit fixtures are useful exploratory stress tests but are excluded
from the confirmatory claims here.

\paragraph{Model and interface scope.}
Only two provider-hosted, rolling model aliases enter the reported confirmatory
comparison.  The results can change with weights, serving stacks, prompts, or
serialization.  E1 changes diagnostic content but does not remove all semantic
differences between messages; E5 matches list structure more closely, yet
neutral placeholders can still affect attention.  Neither contrast identifies
a universal causal quantity called ``feedback quality.''

\paragraph{Resource accounting.}
E4 equalizes the maximum number of model calls, not tokens, latency, energy, or
money.  Early stopping makes realized resources policy-dependent.  A deployment
claim would require reporting both certification rate and the resource measure
that actually matters in that setting.

\paragraph{Artifact status.}
The repository contains the implementation, deterministic fixture generator,
frozen protocol, execution manifest, analysis code, and generated summary
tables used in this draft.  A submission artifact should additionally include
the complete per-round audit logs, a clean-machine reproduction check, and
immutable archival identifiers.  Until that bundle is deposited, we do not
claim that every raw response is publicly archived.

\section{Conclusion}

\toolname{} demonstrates a modest but reusable pattern for computational
theory work: express a scoped physical claim as typed data, expose explicit
necessary obligations, keep a stricter final checker immutable, and record the
result as a certificate whose limitations travel with it.  On the frozen repair
benchmark, this substrate supported both generic retry and more structured
search, while the preferred equal-call policy reversed between the two tested
models.  The scientifically durable object is therefore the exact acceptance
interface and its audit trail, not a universal recipe for how a language model
should exploit it.

The broader \textsc{QFTCert} program is to extend this approach beyond the
present toric-quiver test bed: additional theory schemas, richer operator maps,
independent symbolic backends, and eventually bridges to formal proof systems.
Each extension should preserve the same discipline---state what is checked,
state what remains an assumption, and never promote a finite consistency
certificate into a proof of quantum-field-theory equivalence.

\section{Fixture construction and policy replay}
\label{app:fixtures}

The fixture pipeline is deterministic given its seed and locked configuration.
For each selected theory--node base it generates the target dual description,
applies a candidate corruption, and retains the result only after the three
gates described in Section~\ref{sec:design}.  The committed fixture file,
configuration, prompts, and execution manifest are content-hashed.  This guards
against accidental benchmark drift; it does not prevent overfitting by future
work once the benchmark is public.

The E4 portfolio is computed by deterministic replay rather than by making a
new adaptive API run.  Replaying complete component trajectories makes the
stopping rule auditable and ensures that the comparison does not benefit from a
new model sample.  The replay order, call cap, invalid-output treatment, and
first-certificate stopping rule are fixed in the protocol.

\section{Statistical specification}
\label{app:statistics}

For a given model and endpoint, let $A\in\{0,1\}$ select the two policies and
let $r\in\{1,2,3\}$ index the replication.  The fitted mean model is
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{logit}\{\Pr(Y_{ir}=1)\}
  =\beta_0+\beta_A A+\beta_2\mathbf 1(r=2)+\beta_3\mathbf 1(r=3).
\end{equation}
Fixtures define clusters and an exchangeable working correlation is used across
the repeated policy--replication observations for each fixture.  Inference uses
the robust sandwich covariance.  The reported risk difference averages fitted
probabilities over the locked fixture--replication grid under $A=1$ and $A=0$;
confidence intervals use the delta method.  Holm adjustment is applied to raw
two-sided Wald $p$-values in the families stated in Section~\ref{sec:design}.
Fixture clustering accounts for repeated observations of the same corruption,
but not for possible dependence between distinct corruptions sharing a
theory--node base.  The intervals are therefore conditional benchmark
comparisons and should not be read as sampling uncertainty for a population of
gauge theories.
