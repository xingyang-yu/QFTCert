\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models are increasingly used to carry out multi-step
derivations in theoretical physics, but their output is usually not
checked by any machine. The situation in mathematics is different:
proof assistants provide an exact target for language-model agents to
search against, and systems built on them have progressed from
olympiad-level problem solving
\cite{Yang:2023leandojo,Trinh:2024alphageometry,Hubert:2025alphaproof}
to research-level results, including machine-verified solutions of
decades-open problems \cite{Sothanaphan:2026erdos728} and formally
verified counterexamples \cite{Li:2026disprove}.

However, this approach
cannot be directly copied to physics. Mathematics is built on an
axiomatic foundation, where a statement is settled by a proof. Formal
quantum field theory (QFT) and string theory, although more rigorous
than most other areas of physics, are largely driven by physical
intuition, and many of their central results are not derived from a
fixed set of axioms. In this regime, where experimental data is
typically unavailable, consistency checks are the main tool by which
physicists judge whether a proposal is correct. Seiberg duality
\cite{Seiberg:1994pq,Intriligator:1995au}, which states that two
different four-dimensional $\mathcal{N}=1$ gauge theories flow to the
same infrared physics, is a representative example: there is no proof
assistant that formalizes this statement, and the original evidence for
it consists precisely of such consistency checks. A verifier for this
kind of physics should therefore be built on the consistency checks of
the research field itself, rather than as an imitation of a proof
assistant.

A substantial part of these checks
can be made fully mechanical: whether the 't~Hooft anomalies of a
proposed pair of theories match, whether the superpotential (the
holomorphic interaction terms of the theory) is consistent with the
assigned $R$-charges (the charges under the distinguished $U(1)_R$
symmetry), whether the central charges agree, and whether bounded data
of protected gauge-invariant operators (the chiral ring) coincide. Each
of these is a finite, exact, symbolic computation, and is cheap enough
to run thousands of times.

We present \dualitycert{}, a symbolic verifier based on this layer of
consistency checks. A \emph{candidate duality claim} is an ordered pair
of quiver gauge theories, and \dualitycert{} evaluates four families of
\emph{consistency obligations}: 't~Hooft anomaly matching,
superpotential $R$-charge consistency, central-charge matching, and
$R$-graded bounded chiral-ring consistency. A claim that passes every
obligation receives a \emph{consistency certificate}. The certificate
states that no tested inconsistency was found; it is not a proof of
duality, and a failed obligation rules out the claim only within the
encoded scope. We then use the verifier to construct a repair
environment: an agent is given a deliberately broken candidate claim,
proposes edits, and each attempt is judged by the verifier. The final
acceptance check is stricter than the checks whose outcomes the agent
saw during the interaction.

We use this environment for a preregistered study of how language-model
agents exploit an exact symbolic verifier. The benchmark consists of 145
repairable claims, obtained by perturbing verified dual pairs at depth
one in four classes (deleting a superpotential term, flipping a
coefficient sign, perturbing $R$-charges, and changing a gauge-node
rank), and every model receives exactly the same set of claims. The analysis protocol,
the endpoints, the statistical model, and the multiplicity correction
were frozen before the first confirmatory API call. The confirmatory
analysis covers two publicly available models, \dscode{} and \qwcode{}, with
three replications per policy; effects are estimated by
generalized-estimating-equation (GEE) analysis with fixture-level
clustering under a Holm adjustment and reported as risk differences in
percentage points (pp). Separately, \mmcode{} is evaluated under a
preregistered extension with its own three-hypothesis Holm family
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).

Our contributions are threefold. First, we release \dualitycert{}, the
repair environment, the benchmark, and the frozen protocol. Second,
verifier-gated iteration improves repair success over single-shot
attempts on both confirmatory models ($+8.3$\,pp on \dscode{}, $+7.1$\,pp on
\qwcode{}; Holm-adjusted $p<0.002$). Third, under an equal budget of eleven
verifier-judged calls, the ordering of the two tested exploitation
policies, a stop-first \emph{strategy portfolio} and \emph{independent
verifier-filtered resampling}, reverses between the two named models
($-10.3$\,pp for the portfolio on \dscode{}, $+14.7$\,pp on \qwcode{};
both Holm-significant). Endpoints that isolate the effect of feedback
content show gains only on \qwcode{}: category-level verifier feedback is worth
$+8.7$\,pp over content-free retry, and interpretable obligation
identities are worth $+6.4$\,pp over structurally identical masked
feedback. Our claim is non-universality of the tested policies across
the two evaluated models, not a general classification of models.

The closest related work grounds language-model reasoning in proof
assistants and symbolic engines: AlphaProof couples an agent to a formal
checker \cite{Hubert:2025alphaproof}, LeanDojo opens the proof-assistant
interaction loop to language models \cite{Yang:2023leandojo}, and
AlphaGeometry pairs a generator with a symbolic deduction engine
\cite{Trinh:2024alphageometry}. In parallel, it is known that unaided
self-correction of model reasoning is unreliable
\cite{Huang:2023selfcorrect}, while external checkers help: learned
verifiers rerank candidate solutions \cite{Cobbe:2021verifiers}, and
execution feedback drives program repair \cite{Chen:2023selfdebug}.
\dualitycert{} differs from these works in two ways: the checker is an
exact, domain-specific symbolic verifier for a QFT duality rather than
a learned reward model, and the object under repair is a physics claim
rather than a program or a formal proof.

verifier and the repair interface, Section~3 the benchmark and the
frozen protocol, and Section~4 the confirmatory results. Sections~5
